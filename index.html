Why hexagons?

In 2023, I was chatting with my brother about pathfinding. Dijkstra, A* and their cousins are nearly always explained on square grids. So what happens on hexagons? This snake is my answer, first written in about twelve hours.

No diagonals

On a square grid, a cell has 4 neighbours, or 8 if diagonals are allowed. With 4, paths turn into staircases and many different paths have the same length. With 8, a diagonal step is about 1.41 times longer than a straight one, so the algorithms need special costs.

On a hexagonal grid, the 6 neighbours of a cell are all exactly as far away: there are no diagonals, every step costs the same, and the distance between two cells can be computed exactly.

Dijkstra first

To reach a fruit, the snake started with Dijkstra's algorithm: from its head, it explores the board ring after ring, closest cells first, until it reaches a fruit, then follows the way back. Here every step costs 1, so Dijkstra boils down to a simple breadth-first search.

It always finds the shortest path... but it searches in every direction, even away from the fruit. Turn on View: Explored to see the cells it looks at.

A*: searching in the right direction

A* explores too, but it starts with the cells that look closest to a fruit: the steps already taken, plus the straight distance to the nearest fruit. Walls can only make the real way longer, so this guess never overestimates, and A* still finds the shortest paths.

Results

  • Both always find paths of the same length: on the Path curve, their two lines overlap.
  • On 114 random boards, from 10 to 60 cells wide with up to 35% of walls, A* explored 4,589 cells where Dijkstra explored 44,918: about 10 times fewer.
  • With a wall between the snake and a fruit, Dijkstra explored 368 cells and A* only 60, for the same 13-step path.
  • But A* is not always faster: with hundreds of fruits on the board, it compares every cell it explores with every fruit to find the nearest one, and ends up slower than Dijkstra while exploring less.

Hexagons suit A* well: on an empty board, the hex distance is exactly the length of the shortest path, so A* heads straight for the fruit.

How to go further?

Both algorithms start from scratch at every step, while the board barely changes from one step to the next. Could the snake remember its way?

AW: an aware snake

AW (for aware) runs A* once, remembers the path and follows it. It only searches again when something around it changes:

  • the visitor adds or removes a fruit or a wall;
  • a wall cuts the remembered path, or the fruit it was heading for is gone;
  • the snake is not where AW expected, because it follows another algorithm.

The snake's own moves never block the way ahead: its body only covers cells its head has already crossed. So they don't trigger a new search, with one exception: when its path is a detour, longer than the straight distance to the nearest fruit, and the tail frees a cell right next to the cells the last search explored, a shortcut, or a nearer fruit hidden behind the tail, may have opened up, so AW searches again. Otherwise no shorter way can exist: a straight path can't be beaten, and a shorter way through a freed cell would have to start from a cell that A* explored.

A remembered path could stop being the shortest. These rules are made so that it can't: whatever could shorten it makes AW search again.

In all those games, AW's paths were exactly as short as A*'s. Its time drops even more than its cells: while it keeps its path, it only checks a list of cells, without A*'s calculations.

A first version searched again whenever the tail moved along a detour: around a wall, that was every single step. Watching the explored cells brought it down to 5 searches in 21 steps.

AW#: the same, with numbered cells

AW# is exactly AW: same searches, same explored cells, same paths. Only the code changes.

So far, each cell is named by a text such as "4,2". Every time a search checks a cell, it writes that text again and looks it up in a table. In AW#, a cell is simply a number: on a board of 30, cell (4, 2) is 2 × 30 + 4 = 64, and checking it means reading box 64 of a plain list of numbers. Nothing to write, nothing to look up. Even its neighbours are found with a few additions.

A first test on a board of 60 was promising: the same breadth-first search ran 19 times faster this way. In the comparison, only the Time row should tell AW# from AW.

AW#: results

In every step we compared, nearly 3,000 of them on boards from 10 to 60, AW# made exactly the same choices as AW: the same cells explored, in the same order, and the same paths. And it does it faster:

SituationAW# takes
The whole board explored4.6 to 7.8× less time than A*
Games, open board2.7 to 9.6× less time than AW
Games, 15% walls3.3 to 5.6× less time than AW
Games, 30% walls4.9 to 5.7× less time than AW

Far from the 19 times of the first test: a search does more than look at cells. A* also keeps a heap of the cells to explore, and measures their distance to the fruits.

Two lessons along the way. The first AW# was only 1.5 to 1.8 times faster than A*: swapping two entries of its heap with [a, b] = [b, a] built small arrays at every swap, and a plain temporary variable made it 2.6 times faster. And the timing was unfair at first: AW rebuilt the list of the snake's cells as texts while being timed, when AW# got it ready.
